About

I design systems — and build the pipelines that make them producible.

I came into games through design and stayed because of the machinery underneath it. Across four solo/independent titles and two years inside a live mobile MOBA, the thread in my work has been the same: a design is only as good as the production system that can actually ship it a hundred times over.

That belief pushed me from writing design documents into writing the tools — behaviour trees, blueprint logic, editor scripts, configuration generators — and, more recently, into AI-assisted content pipelines: image generation, image-to-3D, retopology, rigging, and the validation gates that keep generated assets from silently breaking a build.

What I want from a graduate programme is the other half of that loop: the theory, the critical vocabulary, and a studio culture where the question isn't only "can this be produced?" but "what is worth producing?"

How I work

  • Framework first, features second. Structure the system, then fill it.
  • Instrument everything. If I can't measure the experience, I'm guessing.
  • Automate the repetition, never the judgement. AI proposes; a human gate approves.

I · Game Design — 01

Dream

A first-person psychological horror game about reading a building that refuses to explain itself.
Role
Solo — design & all Blueprint implementation
Engine
Unreal Engine 5
Duration
Aug – Dec 2024
Outsourced
Selected art & audio assets only

The core loop

Exploration is the loop; pursuit is a rare branch off it. I deliberately kept the chase scarce — constant threat becomes noise and the player stops feeling it, whereas a threat that has fired once and could fire again turns every ordinary corridor into a possible corridor.

Design decisions

  • The threat is rare on purpose. Pursuit occupies only a small fraction of the running time. Dread does not come from being chased often — it comes from having been chased once and not knowing when it will happen again. Most of the game is the waiting.
  • The AI must be able to lose. The pursuer disengages once the player exits its detection region. A monster that can never be escaped stops being a threat and becomes a timer.
  • State changes are diegetic. Triggering a mechanism swaps the score and shifts the practical lights — the player learns the rule of the space without a single line of UI.
  • Resources meter the courage. A flashlight and a finite stamina bar are the only systems the player manages. Both spend fastest exactly when they are most needed, so caution has a price and panic has a bigger one.
  • Space carries the tension. Narrow corridors and low key-light do the work that jump-scares would otherwise have to do — fear stays sustained rather than spiked.

Production takeaway

Halfway through I stopped adding features and refactored the Blueprint layer, re-grouping scattered logic into explicit categories (Interaction → door / teleport / letter …). Iteration speed roughly doubled: framework first, features second.

I · Game Design — 01 (continued)

Dream — pursuit AI & tension authoring

Pursuer behaviour tree

The Investigate → Disengage branch is the design load-bearing wall. It gives the player a legible escape rule — break line of sight, leave the zone, survive — which converts panic into strategy without lowering the threat.

Narrative, delivered as debris

The story is never narrated; it is left behind. Diary entries carry dates but arrive out of order, so the player assembles a chronology rather than receiving one — which means the horror is something they concluded themselves, and is therefore much harder to argue with.

Authored tension curve

The baseline is not calm — it is a low, continuous unease held by light and geometry alone. Against that floor, the few pursuit events read as enormous. Each one is placed after a long stretch of quiet and set slightly higher than the last, and the recovery troughs shorten as the game goes on. That shortening is what makes the final act feel accelerating even though its content is not denser.

What I would change

The detection region is a hand-placed volume, which made level iteration expensive — moving a doorway meant re-authoring the trigger. A perception-radius + navmesh-query approach would have cost one more day up front and saved a week of tuning.

I · Game Design — 02

0423

A first-person horror game in which a voice that calls itself your host sends you through his building to collect five batteries. He is lying about something. The game never says what.
Role
Solo — design & implementation
Engine
Unreal Engine 5
Duration
Aug – Dec 2024
Interface
A single counter. Nothing else.

Structure

A collect-five errand is the thinnest possible objective, and that is the point: it occupies the part of the player's mind that wants a task, and leaves the rest of their attention free to notice that nothing about the building agrees with the story they were told.

Design decisions

  • One number is the whole HUD. Battery 0 → 5 in the corner, nothing else — no map, no objective list, no health. The player always knows how far along they are and never knows where they are, which is the exact asymmetry the game wants.
  • The narrator is the antagonist. "David" is warm, apologetic, and busy. Framing the errand as hospitality rather than a warning means the dread has to be assembled by the player out of the gap between what he says and what the rooms contain.
  • Red is a state, not a style. The building is lit almost entirely in one hue, so the rare cold or warm room reads as an event. A restricted palette turns lighting into a vocabulary with only two words — and that makes both of them loud.
  • Repetition is the level design. Floors reuse the same corridor grammar with small substitutions. Players stop navigating by landmark and start navigating by memory, which is precisely when a changed room becomes frightening rather than merely noticeable.

What it taught me

Giving the player a trivial task is a stronger horror device than giving them none. With no objective they interrogate the room and find it inert; with a small one they move confidently, and the environment gets to contradict them while their guard is down. The design lever is not the fear — it is where the player's attention is pointed when the fear arrives.

I · Game Design — 03

Jim's Adventure

A 3D action roguelike. Most of the design work sits in one place: a 28-card upgrade deck that has to stay interesting across every run.
Role
Solo — systems, combat & boss design
Engine
Unreal Engine 5 — shipped build
Duration
Jan – May 2024
Focus
Progression pacing & difficulty balance

The deck: 28 cards doing three different jobs

Splitting the deck this way is what stops a run from flattening. Unlock cards change the verb set, so they matter most early; reshape cards change how a verb behaves and only pay off once you own it; tune cards are the filler that makes the other two feel like a find. Shipping most effects at two magnitudes buys a rarity axis without building a rarity system.

Dynamic difficulty

  • Enemy attack and HP scale by +20 % of current value every minute. Compounding, not linear — so the pressure curve matches the exponential shape of the player's own card stacking.
  • It exists to kill farming, not the player. Without it the optimal strategy is to stall in a safe corner and out-level the content, which quietly deletes the challenge.
  • Result: runs converged to a 12–18 minute band, the Dragon reachable but never guaranteed — the target shape for a roguelike.

Encounter roster & gating

The bestiary is built as silhouette classes, not stat blocks — bat, spider in two sizes, skull, golem, wizard, dragon — so a threat is read by shape at distance. Wizards ship as elemental variants sharing one skeleton but firing different projectiles: three encounter types for the cost of one character. Guardians are parallel, not sequential — the player picks the order, but all must fall before the Dragon unlocks.

Impact follows the same logic — camera shake is three named tiers, not a per-effect tuning job, so every new ability inherits a consistent weight.

I · Game Design — 04

Clay Beats

A couch co-op rhythm game about two cats learning pottery. Shipped on Steam. I owned animation production and core interaction design.
Role
Animation production · core interaction design
Team
Shatter Studios LLC — university capstone team
Release
24 March 2025 · Steam · free-to-play
Format
Local co-op, two controllers · 7 levels

Core interaction

  • Two players, one artefact. The design problem that defines this game is that both players act on the same pot. Individual accuracy had to stay legible while the outcome stayed shared, so each player gets a private combo readout and the pot on the wheel carries the joint result.
  • Feedback must beat the score. Players judge themselves on what they see and hear long before they read a number, so each input state — early, perfect, late, miss — has its own animation and audio signature. The character's recovery timing is the judgement display.
  • Failure had to stay musical. A missed beat deforms the pot and thins the arrangement instead of halting the run. Losing should still sound like a song and look like a lump of clay — which is also the joke.

Animation production

Built and cleaned the animation set end to end — blocking in Maya, mocap cleanup and retiming in MotionBuilder, rig retargeting where library motion was viable. The binding constraint was beat-locking: across seven original songs every clip had to land its accent on frame-exact musical time, so clips were authored against a tempo grid rather than to taste. A clip that reads beautifully and lands two frames late is a bug in a rhythm game.

Why this project matters to me

It was the first time I shipped to a public storefront rather than to a grader — a real store page, real strangers, real reviews. Almost everything I now believe about production I learned here under a hard deadline with a team: that scope is a schedule problem, that a pipeline is worth more than a hero asset, and that "done" means a build other people can run.

Transferable outcome

The tempo-grid authoring method built here is the direct ancestor of the animation state-machine and blend-timing work I later did on a live commercial title — see Section II.

II · Systems Design in Production — 05

Character presentation state machine

Sole owner of the animation state architecture behind a collectible companion system in a live mobile MOBA.
Context
Tencent TiMi Studio Group · Systems Designer
Title
Nation-wide mobile MOBA (live service)
Period
Aug 2025 – present
Ownership
Sole designer for the state architecture

Under NDA  No proprietary screenshots, configuration data or engine files are shown. All diagrams in Section II are re-drawn by me at a generic, non-identifying level of abstraction.

The problem

Out-of-match companions have to idle, wander, react to the player, respond to taps, and play one-off performance beats — while the player is simultaneously browsing menus, changing outfits and entering queue. The failure mode is not a missing animation; it is a bad seam: a companion snapping mid-stride, or freezing because two systems each believed they owned the character that frame.

The architecture

  • Explicit single ownership. Exactly one state drives the character each frame; every other system requests rather than sets. This removed the entire class of "two systems fighting" bugs.
  • Transitions are content, not glue. Each edge has its own authored blend-out, so a walk that is interrupted by a tap decelerates instead of cutting.
  • Interrupts resolve by declared priority. A reaction can always take the character from idle or move, but never from a one-shot performance mid-beat.

Outcome

Out-of-match presentation reads as continuous motion rather than a sequence of clips. Concretely: no more visible pops at the idle↔move seam, and reaction beats can be triggered from any menu context without the animation team hand-authoring a case for each one.

What I actually did

  • Enumerated every behaviour the feature promised, then cut the state list to the smallest set that could express all of them.
  • Wrote the transition matrix — including the illegal transitions, which is where most of the design lives.
  • Specified blend timings against the animation team's clips rather than assuming defaults.
  • Sat in review with engineering and animation until the seam list was empty.

Design principle carried forward

A state machine is a contract about who is allowed to lie to the player. Writing down which transitions are forbidden turned out to be far more valuable than enumerating the permitted ones.

Wardrobe & character customisation systems

Co-owned delivery of a cosmetic wardrobe and a player-facing character-creation flow, from feature spec through resource integration to launch.

Under NDA  Diagrams are generic re-drawings; no product UI is reproduced.

The combinatorial problem

  • Rules beat exceptions. Instead of blacklisting broken pairs one by one, we defined compatibility classes so a new item inherits its constraints on arrival.
  • Defaults carry the experience. Most players never open the deep customisation tree, so the default and the first-time-user preset were treated as designed content, not fallbacks.
  • Resource integration is design work. Naming conventions, LOD rules and slot metadata decide whether the art team can ship an item in a day or a week.

Delivery pipeline I worked inside

My contribution

  • Owned the main flow for character creation and outfit combination alongside a second designer.
  • Wrote the configuration schema so that art deliverables and design intent had a single source of truth.
  • Drove resource integration and the pre-launch combination sweep that protects the first-run experience.

What I learned about live products

Cosmetic systems look like content but behave like infrastructure. The right question at spec time was never "what should this outfit look like" — it was "what will it cost to add the two-hundredth one." That framing is what I want to bring into a research-oriented design programme: treating production capacity itself as a design material.

II · Systems Design in Production — 07

Gameplay-mode logic: skills, monster AI, boss phases

Adapting a competitive hero roster into an entertainment battle-royale mode — and rebuilding the enemy AI that populates it.

Under NDA  Structures below are re-drawn generically; no proprietary logic assets are reproduced.

Re-tuning skills for a different contract

A competitive MOBA kit is balanced for a 15-minute macro game. Dropped into a fast entertainment mode, the same kit reads as sluggish — long cooldowns and resource costs that exist to create macro decisions instead just create waiting. I worked through the roster adjusting cooldowns, costs and trigger conditions via hero logic graphs and buff triggers so the kits kept their identity while matching the new tempo.

The rule I worked to

Change the rhythm, never the fantasy. A player who mains a hero should still recognise their hero; what changes is how often they get to be them.

Where the AI actually lived

A significant finding during this work: the monster AI I was asked to modify was not driven by the visual logic graphs at all, but by behaviour-tree assets referenced by a path field in the configuration table. Edits to the graphs had no effect — the graphs were vestigial. Tracing the real execution path before changing anything saved the rest of the schedule.

Transferable lesson

In a decade-old live codebase, the documented system and the running system are different systems. I now verify which one I'm editing before I touch it — usually by instrumenting a probe and watching the log, not by reading the code.

Boss encounter: phase state machine

  • Transformation is a buff, not a new entity. Driving phase changes through the buff layer keeps threat, targeting and aggro state intact across the transition, so the encounter never resets its social contract with the players fighting it.
  • Reuse the proven AI, replace the form. Cloning an existing, battle-tested behaviour tree and changing the presentation per phase produced a stable boss far faster than hand-authoring a new AI graph — an approach I arrived at after a hand-built version repeatedly deadlocked.
  • Fail loudly. The debugging discipline that came out of this: a boss that gets stuck in idle is a design bug with an engineering signature, and it is found by reading timestamps, not by intuition.

The design problem was never generation speed. It was building a validation gate I could trust.

Pipeline architecture

The actual insight

Generation was the cheap part. The expensive part was answering "how do I know it isn't wrong?" at a scale where I can no longer read every entry. So the design work went into the gate: a machine-checkable definition of what "correct" means for one entry, applied to all of them.

Design rules that made it work

  • Never hand-patch a generated artefact. A one-off fix hides a systematic defect; the failure has to be pushed back into the pattern so it is fixed 110 times.
  • Encode the traps. Every production landmine I hit (a path field that silently needs a file extension, an index that must equal its own position, a byte-order marker that crashes a loader) went into a written constraint list the pipeline checks against.
  • Keep a human at the end. The gate rejects; only I approve. Nothing generated reached the build without review.

Why this is a design skill, not a tooling skill

Defining the validation gate is writing down what the design actually requires — most of what a spec leaves implicit becomes explicit the moment a machine has to check it. The pipeline was, in effect, the most rigorous design document I have ever written.

A character art pipeline for Roblox

Concept → design sheet → modular parts → generated 3D → R15 rig → in-engine. Built as a repeatable route, not a one-off character.
Role
Pipeline design & end-to-end execution
Toolchain
Image gen · image→3D · Maya · Roblox Studio
Output
Rigged, skinned, engine-ready R15 character
Documented
Published as a public process write-up

The pipeline

The reason to formalise this as a pipeline rather than "make a character" is that the hard problem in generative production is consistency across stages. Each handoff is an opportunity for the model to reinterpret the design; putting an explicit reference artefact at the top and checking every stage against it is what keeps stage five looking like stage one.

Why modular parts

Generating a whole dressed character in one pass gives you an un-editable blob. Decomposing into hair / garment / accessory / footwear and generating each separately produces parts that can be swapped, re-skinned and re-used across characters — which is the difference between an asset and a wardrobe.

Constraints that shaped it

  • Roblox R15 is a fixed skeleton — the art has to come to the rig, never the reverse.
  • Custom characters bind as accessories rather than custom rigs, which keeps every stock animation working for free.
  • Poly and texture budgets are strict, so retopology is a required stage, not an optimisation.

Modular parts & generated geometry

Image → 3D geometry, part by part

A technique worth documenting: layered garments

Generating a dressed body as one mesh looks correct and deforms badly — the garment inherits the body's skin weights and shears at the shoulder. Splitting into base body + garment shell + isolated sleeves lets each layer take its own weights, so the silhouette survives an arm raise. This is the kind of finding that only appears once you push a generated asset all the way through rigging rather than stopping at a nice render.

Failure modes I catalogued

  • Thin shells. Hair and cloth edges generate as zero-thickness and vanish under normal-based shading — solved by thickening before generation, not after.
  • Hidden-object export loss. Objects hidden in the outliner silently fail to export, producing a rig with no skeleton and no error message.
  • Scale drift. Each generated part returns in its own units; a normalisation step against the rig had to become mandatory rather than remembered.

Method over output

Every failure above became a written check in the pipeline documentation. The deliverable of this project is not the character — it is the list.

Assembly, rigging & in-engine result

The decision that made this reusable

Binding custom characters as accessories on the stock R15 rig — rather than authoring a custom rig per character — means every animation in the platform's library keeps working, and a new character costs art time only. For a project that needs many characters, this single constraint is worth more than any individual asset quality gain.

Where the human stays

Generation handled bulk geometry. Proportion, silhouette correction, weight painting and the final in-engine read were all hand work — and they are exactly the stages where the character stops being plausible and starts being appealing. The pipeline's job is to buy time for those stages, not to replace them.

Image → 3D → game-ready asset

Automating the route from a single reference image to a retopologised, PBR-textured, rigged model — and knowing exactly where the automation has to stop.

Pipeline & where the human intervenes

  • Generation is asynchronous by nature. These are long jobs, so the middleware is a submit / poll / fetch loop with signed requests and back-off retry — transient overload is an expected state, not an error.
  • One interface, many vendors. Different generation services disagree on formats, auth and field names; normalising them behind a single call surface keeps that mess out of the artist's day.
  • Everything lands in a fixed archive layout (project / character / part), because an asset you cannot find is an asset you will regenerate.

Generated PBR channel set

Also in this toolchain

ComfyUI deployed locally on a 12 GB consumer GPU, running a full custom-node workflow — which forces real engagement with VRAM limits, batching and quantisation trade-offs rather than treating generation as an infinite cloud resource.

The honest assessment

These tools get an asset to roughly 70 % in minutes and then stop. The remaining 30 % — silhouette correction, topology for deformation, weight painting — is where a model is either good or unusable, and it is still entirely human. My interest is in designing the handoff, not in pretending it doesn't exist.

Three decisions I would defend

  • Money is never a float. All amounts stored as strings and computed as decimals, behind a single wallet service that every balance change must pass through, with a signed ledger entry per movement. The system is auditable by construction rather than by discipline.
  • The deploy gate is the quality bar. 70 smoke tests run in CI on every push; the deploy only fires if all pass. Not "run the tests" — red means it does not ship.
  • AI proposes, a human commits. To speed up settlement I added a vision model that reads result screenshots. Its output goes to a staging buffer and a confirmation dialog — the model never writes to the database. Low confidence returns "unknown" and settles nothing.

Design lesson

Real users double-click. Handling that took three layers — front-end submit locking, a server-side duplicate window, and a database uniqueness constraint as the final guarantee. A design that only works when the user behaves is not a design.

Architecture

The analytics loop

I designed the event-tracking taxonomy alongside the features, so behaviour data existed from day one. It showed which pages users actually reached (averaging 6.26 active pages each) and drove the next round of navigation and UI changes. Instrumenting at design time rather than after launch is the single practice I would carry into any product I work on.

Second Brain — a self-organising knowledge system

A personal research project: a retrieval system that keeps learning, de-duplicating and correcting itself, built as a graph rather than a list.

Structure

Flat vector search returns things that are similar. Building explicit connections between memories, then distilling densely-connected clusters into higher-order insights, lets the system return things that are relevant but not obviously similar — which is where most useful retrieval actually lives.

Two ideas I am proud of

1 · A quarantine before the library

Anything newly acquired lands in a staging area, not the main store. It is promoted only after passing a quality gate — length, source credibility, and near-duplicate similarity against existing memories. Most systems optimise retrieval; almost none defend ingestion. A knowledge base degrades from what you let in.

2 · A retrieval cascade, cheapest first

Queries try the existing memory store, then a local document sweep, and only then reach the open web. Most questions are answered before the third tier — which saves time, cost, and the temptation to trust a stranger's page over your own recorded experience.

Why a designer built this

It started as a practical problem — hundreds of scattered production notes I could no longer search — but the interesting part turned out to be an interface question: what should a system remember on your behalf, and how does it show you that it might be wrong? That is the research question I would most like to pursue formally.

V · Original Visual Work — 14

Chinese Dreamcore

A personal image series about the interiors of a 1990s–2000s Chinese childhood, made as environment research for an unannounced narrative project.

The design problem: a series, not a picture

Twenty images that each look good but do not look like the same place are worthless as environment research. The entire craft here is holding a world constant across a set: fixed lens language, fixed time of day, a restricted palette, and a locked reference that every generation is conditioned against. It is the same consistency problem as the character pipeline in Section III, in a different medium.

Why this subject

Dreamcore usually borrows a Western vocabulary — motel corridors, liminal pools. I wanted to know whether the same uncanny register could be built from specifically Chinese domestic memory: the tiled entryway, the exercise-book-covered desk, the courtyard at dusk. The interesting finding is that the unease does not come from distortion at all. It comes from emptiness plus accuracy — a room rendered correctly, with the person who should be in it missing.

Where it is going

This set is environment research for a narrative project in the lineage of Dream and 0423 — a game about a house you have to read. The images are the art-direction bible, not the final art.
